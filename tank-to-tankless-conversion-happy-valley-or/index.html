Tank to Tankless Conversion in Happy Valley, OR: What to Know

Tankless water heaters promise endless hot water, but a conversion changes more than the heater. Here is what to check before you commit.

By the Happy Valley 24/7 Plumbing teamPublished September 24, 2026Updated November 13, 2026

Converting from a tank to a tankless water heater is worth it for some homes and a poor value for others. The honest answer depends on three things: whether your gas line and venting can support a tankless unit without major work, how your household uses hot water, and how long you plan to stay. This guide walks through each so you can decide before anyone starts cutting pipe.

A tankless heater heats water only when you open a tap. There is no tank to lose heat or to rust through, so units tend to last longer and take less space. The trade-off is a larger up-front cost and, in many homes, a list of supporting changes that the unit price does not show.

What changes in a conversion

A tank heater and a tankless heater need different things from the house. The tankless unit burns far more gas at once, so the existing gas pipe may be too small. It usually needs a different vent, often a sealed plastic vent in place of the old metal flue. Condensing models produce acidic condensate that needs a drain. Electric units demand a heavy circuit that many panels cannot spare.

ItemTank heaterTankless heater
Gas demandModerateHigh, needs larger pipe
VentingMetal flue to the roofSealed plastic or special vent
CondensateNoneDrain needed on condensing models
FootprintFloor space, a panWall mounted
MaintenanceFlush, check anodeAnnual descaling, filter cleaning

Who benefits most

Households that run hot water steadily across the day see a clear benefit, because the unit never reheats a tank in the background. Homes that need floor space, such as a utility closet being reclaimed, benefit from wall mounting. Long-term owners benefit from a service life that is often longer than a tank's. Households with very light hot water use may never recover the extra cost.

Where conversions go wrong

Most disappointment comes from sizing. A single unit sized for one shower will struggle when two showers and a dishwasher run together. Another trap is skipping the gas line check, which leaves the heater starved at peak demand and throwing error codes. A third is forgetting maintenance, which brings scale and sensor problems. A good plumber raises these before the sale, not after the install.

Questions to settle first

  • How many fixtures run at the same time in your busiest hour?
  • What is the gas pipe size from the meter to the heater location?
  • Where will the vent exit, and are clearances to windows met?
  • Is there a drain for condensate near the unit?
  • Does the electrical panel have capacity if the unit is electric?

What it costs, and what to compare

Compare the full installed price, not the unit price. A tankless heater costs more than a tank, and a conversion adds pipe, venting, condensate and sometimes electrical work. Over time, you may save on energy and avoid a tank replacement every decade or so, but the payback is measured in years, not months.

Our tankless water heater page lists what goes into a quote, and a plumber can walk through your numbers.

If your current tank is failing and you need hot water now, a standard water heater replacement may be the right short-term move, with a tankless conversion planned later.

Happy Valley note: soft water and permits

Sunrise Water Authority, which serves Happy Valley, describes its water as generally soft at about 1.2 grains per gallon and drawn mainly from the Clackamas River. For a tankless unit, that is good news, since mineral scale builds slowly on the heat exchanger. Annual flushing still matters for sensors and the inlet screen.

Permit requirements for a conversion vary by project, and the Happy Valley Building Division administers the state's plumbing and gas codes locally, so ask them about your specific scope.

Planning the project

Start with a plumber's site visit. The visit should include a look at the gas meter and line, the vent path, the electrical panel if relevant and the water heater location. Ask for a written scope that lists the unit, vent, gas, condensate, electrical and permit items.

If you are in Damascus on a larger lot, longer gas runs can change the answer. If you are in Clackamas or Damascus, tell us when you call so the plumber arrives with the right information.

After the install

Plan an annual flush and filter cleaning, and keep the manual where you can find it. Note the error codes your unit shows and what they mean. A small habit, such as running a faucet briefly before a shower, helps units with a minimum flow requirement perform well. If you notice cold bursts or codes, a service visit under our tankless page is the quick fix.

A realistic payback conversation

Payback is where conversions are most often oversold. Savings come from lower standby loss and, over time, from avoiding a tank replacement every decade or so. Whether that offsets the higher installed price depends on your hot water use and your fuel costs, and it can take many years.

Ask the plumber to put the installed price of both options side by side, and weigh the savings you can actually expect against what you value, such as space and endless hot water.

Choosing an installer

Look for an installer who asks questions before quoting. They should want to see the gas meter, the heater location and the electrical panel. A quote that arrives without a visit is guessing at the hidden costs. Ask for the scope in writing, including venting, gas piping, condensate handling and permit steps, and ask what warranty applies to the labor as well as the unit.
