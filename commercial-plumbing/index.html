Commercial plumbing differs from residential work in scale and stakes. Pipes are larger, fixtures serve more people, and downtime costs money. A flooded restroom during lunch rush or a kitchen drain backing up at dinner service needs a quick response and a clear plan. Call, tell us what is affected, and we will schedule a plumber who can diagnose and fix the problem with minimal disruption.

If the problem is a single backed-up line, our drain cleaning techniques apply at larger scale. When a main line is involved, we treat it as a commercial sewer line cleaning job and work out whether the blockage is yours or on the public main.

Emergency needs for businesses

Typical urgent calls include backed-up restroom or kitchen drains, floor drains overflowing, burst supply lines, water heater failures, toilet and urinal malfunctions and a leak that threatens inventory. For any of these, shut off water at the nearest valve if you can, protect electrical equipment and call. If the building has a fire sprinkler or backflow device, mention it so the plumber knows what is on site.

Business owner checklist while you wait

  • Close the nearest isolation valve or the main, and tell staff where they are.
  • Move inventory and electronics away from standing water.
  • Block off affected restrooms and post a sign.
  • Take photos and note times for your insurance file.

Kitchens and grease

Restaurants fill drains with fats, oils and grease. Grease interceptors and traps are meant to catch it, but they need regular cleaning. A neglected trap lets grease reach the main line, which is why repeat kitchen backups often lead to hydro jetting and a maintenance schedule. We discuss trap condition, sizing and local requirements rather than guessing, and we point you to the agency that sets rules where that matters.

Multi-unit and office buildings

Apartment and office buildings share risers, stacks and sewer laterals. A blockage in one unit may be a shared line issue, and a leak in one place can damage others. The plumber traces which fixtures are affected, isolates the problem and fixes it without shutting off more of the building than needed. We also handle water heater replacement, backflow device questions and fixture replacement across multiple units.

What Happy Valley businesses should know

Commercial areas around Happy Valley include retail centers, offices and restaurants, and many were built in the last two decades. That means modern piping but heavy fixture use, so wear shows up in valves, flush valves and drains rather than in corroded pipe.

Public sewer and stormwater service is managed by Water Environment Services, and tenants should know which party, owner or tenant, is responsible for each part of the system.

Permits and inspections for larger work go through the building department, and rules vary by scope. We describe what a job involves in plain terms so you can ask the right questions.

Planned maintenance

A planned visit costs less than an emergency. Many businesses schedule drain maintenance, water heater service and backflow device checks. Leak detection helps when a utility bill rises without explanation. Ask us about a maintenance plan that fits your hours so work happens outside peak times.

Pricing

You hear the price before work starts. Commercial jobs are priced by scope, access, equipment and time. If the work will take more than one visit, the plumber explains the schedule and any temporary measures to keep you open.

What to do before we arrive at a business

  • Close the building main or the affected branch valve and tell staff which fixtures to avoid.
  • Stop using floor drains and kitchen sinks if grease or sewage is backing up.
  • Keep customers away from wet floors and anything electrical.
  • Note any health department or landlord reporting duties, since those clocks start when the problem does.

Commercial work follows the Oregon Plumbing Specialty Code (OPSC), built on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC). Grease interceptor and backflow rules vary by sewer and water provider, so confirm requirements with Water Environment Services and the building department.
